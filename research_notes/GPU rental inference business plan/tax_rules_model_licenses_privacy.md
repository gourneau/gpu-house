# Tax rules, model licenses, and data-privacy rules for a California sole-proprietor GPU inference business started in late 2026

Scope: US federal and California tax rules for a cash-basis Schedule C business owned by a high-income California W-2 employee; commercial-use terms of popular open-weight LLMs; privacy, logging, and export-control rules for an inference provider. Every URL below was checked on 2026-10-22. Quotes are verbatim from the cited page. Items marked "estimate" are the researcher's arithmetic or inference, not a quoted rule. "Not found" means the research did not locate the item. The owner's CPA should confirm everything before filing.

Reading key: "How it applies" lines are the researcher's inference for this business, not a quote.

## Q1. Cash-method deduction timing for rented compute (Reg. 1.461-1(a)(1), Pub 538, Pub 334)

### Takeaway
A cash-basis sole proprietor deducts a cloud or GPU rental bill in the year it is actually paid, unless the payment creates a benefit that extends "substantially beyond" the close of the year. Month-to-month or in-arrears cloud bills paid in 2026 are 2026 deductions. Prepayments are governed by Q2.

### Cited Findings
- Reg. 1.461-1(a)(1): "Under the cash receipts and disbursements method of accounting, amounts representing allowable deductions shall, as a general rule, be taken into account for the taxable year in which paid." It continues: "If an expenditure results in the creation of an asset having a useful life which extends substantially beyond the close of the taxable year, such an expenditure may not be deductible, or may be deductible only in part, for the taxable year in which made." [26 CFR 1.461-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.461-1) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRS Pub 538, Cash Method, Expenses: "Under the cash method, generally, you deduct expenses in the tax year in which you actually pay them." and "However, you may not be able to deduct an expense paid in advance." [IRS Pub 538](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p538) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRS Pub 538, Expense paid in advance: "An expense you pay in advance is deductible only in the year to which it applies," unless "the expense qualifies for the 12-month rule." [IRS Pub 538](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p538) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRS Pub 334 (Tax Guide for Small Business), Line F accounting method: "If you use the cash method, show all items of taxable income actually or constructively received during the year". Pub 334 lists "Expenses" and "Expenses paid in advance" under its Cash Method section; the body text was not reachable in the fetch window. [IRS Pub 334](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Schedule C instructions, Line F: "If you use the cash method, show all items of taxable income actually or constructively received during the year". [IRS Schedule C instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- How it applies: A GPU rental invoice paid by card or ACH in December 2026 for December 2026 usage is deductible in 2026. A bill paid in January 2027 for December 2026 usage is deductible in 2027, not 2026, because cash-basis timing follows payment, not usage.
- The business must also have begun (see Q9). Compute paid for before the business begins is a start-up expenditure under IRC 195, not a current 162 deduction.

### Gaps
- The Pub 334 body text for "Expenses paid in advance" was not reachable; Pub 538 covers the same rule and is cited instead.

## Q2. The 12-month rule for prepaid expenses (Treas. Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)); Zaninovich; prepaid cloud or SaaS

### Takeaway
A 12-month GPU reservation paid in full and non-refundable in December 2026 with service starting in December 2026 satisfies the 12-month rule and is deductible by a cash-basis taxpayer in 2026. If service instead starts January 1, 2027 and runs 12 months to December 31, 2027, the rule is still met (the benefit does not extend beyond the end of 2027). If service starts later in January 2027 (for example January 15, 2027) and runs 12 months into January 2028, the benefit extends beyond the end of the year following payment, the rule fails, and the payment must be capitalized and deducted over the service period.

### Cited Findings
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(1), the rule: "Except as otherwise provided in this paragraph (f), a taxpayer is not required to capitalize under this section amounts paid to create (or to facilitate the creation of) any right or benefit for the taxpayer that does not extend beyond the earlier of— (i) 12 months after the first date on which the taxpayer realizes the right or benefit; or (ii) The end of the taxable year following the taxable year in which the payment is made." [26 CFR 1.263(a)-4 (eCFR)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFR9b4e8d8fb4d5b2b/section-1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22); mirror at [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(d)(3) treats prepaid expenses as created intangibles that must be capitalized "Except as provided in paragraph (f) of this section (relating to the 12-month rule)". Example 2 under (d)(3): "X corporation, a cash method taxpayer, enters into a 24-month lease of office space. At the time of the lease signing, X prepays $240,000. No other amounts are due under the lease. The $240,000 is a prepaid expense and must be capitalized under this paragraph (d)(3)." [26 CFR 1.263(a)-4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(8), Example 1: a $10,000 premium paid December 1, 2005 for a 1-year policy beginning February 1, 2006: "Because the right or benefit attributable to the $10,000 payment extends beyond the end of the taxable year following the taxable year in which the payment is made, the 12-month rule provided by this paragraph (f) does not apply. N must capitalize the $10,000 payment." Example 2: same facts with a term beginning December 15, 2005: "The 12-month rule of this paragraph (f) applies to the $10,000 payment because the right or benefit attributable to the payment neither extends more than 12 months beyond December 15, 2005 (the first date the benefit is realized by the taxpayer) nor beyond the end of the taxable year following the taxable year in which the payment is made. Accordingly, N is not required to capitalize the $10,000 payment." [eCFR 1.263(a)-4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFR9b4e8d8fb4d5b2b/section-1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(4): the rule does not apply to rights of indefinite duration, including "a right attributable to an agreement to provide or receive a fixed amount of goods or services." [eCFR 1.263(a)-4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFR9b4e8d8fb4d5b2b/section-1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(5): "the duration of a right includes any renewal period if all of the facts and circumstances in existence during the taxable year in which the right is created indicate a reasonable expectancy of renewal." [eCFR 1.263(a)-4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFR9b4e8d8fb4d5b2b/section-1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(6), coordination with section 461: "In the case of a taxpayer using an accrual method of accounting, the rules of this paragraph (f) do not affect the determination of whether a liability is incurred during the taxable year, including the determination of whether economic performance has occurred". No parallel limit is stated for cash-method taxpayers. [eCFR 1.263(a)-4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFR9b4e8d8fb4d5b2b/section-1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(7): "A taxpayer may elect not to apply the rule contained in paragraph (f)(1)" by capitalizing on a timely filed return. [eCFR 1.263(a)-4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFR9b4e8d8fb4d5b2b/section-1.263(a)-4) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRS Pub 538 restates the test for cash-method taxpayers: "Under the 12-month rule, a taxpayer is not required to capitalize amounts paid to create certain rights or benefits" that "do not extend beyond the earlier of" "12 months after the right or benefit begins," or "The end of the tax year after the tax year in which payment is made." Pub 538 Example 2: a calendar-year taxpayer who pays $10,000 on July 1, 2021 for a one-year policy starting that day deducts the full $10,000 in 2021. [IRS Pub 538](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p538) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Zaninovich v. Commissioner, 616 F.2d 429 (9th Cir. 1980): cash-basis farm partnership paid $27,000 on December 20, 1973 for the lease year December 1, 1973 to November 30, 1974 and deducted it all in 1973. The court held: "We hold that the payment in this case was fully deductible in the year of payment." It adopted the one-year rule: "we apply the 'one-year rule' in this case and hold that the December, 1973 payment was fully deductible in the year of payment." On distortion of income: "There was, however, no contention or evidence at trial that there was either a distortion of income or an attempt by the taxpayers here to evade taxes in some way. Moreover, if there was a distortion of income in every situation in which an accounting method did not precisely match items of expense and income, the cash basis method would not be a permissible method of accounting, as a certain degree of imprecision is built into the method." Footnote 7 preserves the Commissioner's section 446 power: "The Commissioner has broad discretion under § 446 to modify a taxpayer's accounting method to avoid gross distortions and to insure a clear reflection of income." [Zaninovich, 616 F.2d 429 (OpenJurist)](https://openjurist.org/616/f2d/429) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Zaninovich distinguished pure "advance payment" cases: "The cases involving substantially shorter terms in which taxpayers have been required to amortize have, unlike the case before us, involved advance payments, see, e. g., Williamson v. Commissioner, 37 T.C. 941 (1962) (payment on December 27, 1956 for a lease running for one year from March 1, 1957 where payment was not due until beginning of lease)". [Zaninovich (OpenJurist)](https://openjurist.org/616/f2d/429) (checked 2026-10-22)
- The Tax Court reads Zaninovich as limited to cash-basis taxpayers: in Lattice Semiconductor Corp., T.C. Memo. 2011-100, "the Ninth Circuit specifically distinguished between an accrual-basis taxpayer (such as Lattice) and a cash-basis taxpayer (such as the one in Zaninovich). Thus, the Tax Court found that Zaninovich applies only to cash-basis taxpayers". [The Tax Adviser, July 2011](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2011/jul/taxtrends-jul11-story-01/) (checked 2026-10-22)
- The 12-month rule regulations were finalized in T.D. 9107 (2004), "effective for amounts paid or incurred on or after December 31, 2003." [The Tax Adviser, July 2011](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2011/jul/taxtrends-jul11-story-01/) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Prepaid cloud or SaaS under the 12-month rule: a web search found only general practitioner commentary (a guide noting a 5-year software license must be capitalized, and a two-year computer maintenance contract example that fails the rule). No IRS ruling, notice, or case specific to prepaid cloud computing or SaaS was found. [BNN CPA, Accelerating Your Deduction for Prepaid Expenses](https://www.bnncpa.com/resources/accelerating-your-deduction-for-prepaid-expenses/) (checked 2026-10-22, secondary)

### Inferences
- Scenario A, pay in full December 2026, non-refundable, service begins December 2026, 12-month term: the benefit ends in December 2027. That is within 12 months of the first day of service and before December 31, 2027. Both prongs are met. Deduct in 2026 (Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(1), Reg. 1.461-1(a)(1), Pub 538, Zaninovich).
- Scenario B, pay December 2026, service begins January 1, 2027 for 12 months ending December 31, 2027: 12 months after the first benefit date is January 1, 2028; the end of the taxable year following payment is December 31, 2027. The earlier date is December 31, 2027. The benefit ends December 31, 2027 and so "does not extend beyond" it. The rule is met and the cash-basis deduction falls in 2026, the year paid. Estimate: this is the researcher's reading of (f)(1)(ii) and reg Example 2; the CPA should confirm.
- Scenario C, pay December 2026, service begins January 15, 2027 for 12 months ending January 14, 2028: the benefit extends beyond December 31, 2027. The rule fails (same pattern as reg Example 1). Capitalize and deduct ratably over the service months (2027 and 2028).
- A term longer than 12 months, an auto-renewing contract with a "reasonable expectancy of renewal," or a contract for a fixed quantity of GPU-hours rather than a fixed time period can fall outside the rule under (f)(4) and (f)(5). Write the reservation as a fixed 12-month period, not a block of hours.
- A December payment that is not actually due until January is the "advance payment" fact pattern Zaninovich distinguished (Williamson). A contract that requires full non-refundable payment at signing avoids that distinction. The payment must be a real payment, not a refundable deposit.
- The distortion-of-income caveat: section 446(b) lets the IRS require a method that clearly reflects income. Zaninovich says ordinary cash-method mismatch is not distortion. A one-time December prepayment that is small relative to the owner's total income is low risk; the risk rises if the prepayment is large and timed only to shift income between years.

### Gaps
- Rev. Rul. 79-229 (cash-basis prepaid farm supplies three-part test) is often cited for cash-method prepayments; it was not retrieved in this research. The CPA may want to check it.
- No IRS guidance specific to prepaid cloud compute was found. Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(7) mentions "non-renewable, 12-month service contracts" as an example of contracts to which the rule applies, which is the closest regulatory language.

## Q3. Bonus depreciation in 2026 (IRC 168(k) as amended by P.L. 119-21); servers as 5-year property; placed in service

### Takeaway
IRC 168(k)(1)(A) now provides a 100 percent first-year allowance for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025 and placed in service in the tax year. Servers and GPUs are "computer or peripheral equipment," which is "qualified technological equipment," which is 5-year property. The deduction belongs to the year the server is placed in service, meaning ready and available for its assigned function, so a server racked, powered, and able to serve by December 31, 2026 qualifies for 2026 even if no customer has used it yet.

### Cited Findings
- IRC 168(k)(1)(A) as amended: "the depreciation deduction provided by section 167(a) for the taxable year in which such property is placed in service shall include an allowance equal to 100 percent of the adjusted basis of the qualified property". [26 USC 168](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 168(k)(2)(A): qualified property is property "to which this section applies which has a recovery period of 20 years or less" and "the original use of which begins with the taxpayer or the acquisition of which by the taxpayer meets the requirements of clause (i) of subparagraph (E)" (used property rules). [26 USC 168](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Effective date, P.L. 119-21 §70301(c): "the amendments made by this section ... shall apply to property acquired after January 19, 2025." and "(4) Acquisition date determination.—For purposes of paragraph (1), property shall not be treated as acquired after the date on which a written binding contract is entered into for such acquisition." [26 USC 168, notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)
- The old phase-down paragraphs (k)(6) and (k)(8) now read "Repealed. Pub. L. 119–21, title VII, § 70301(b)(1)(B), July 4, 2025". A transitional election in (k)(10) lets a taxpayer use 40 percent instead of 100 percent for "the first taxable year ending after January 19, 2025". [26 USC 168](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRS Notice 2026-11 (January 14, 2026) gives interim guidance "on the permanent 100% additional first-year depreciation deduction under section 168(k) for eligible depreciable property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, as reinstated by Pub. L. No. 119-21". It says taxpayers "should rely on the acquisition date rules in the section 168(k) regulations finalized after the enactment of" the TCJA, "including in determining whether property is acquired or constructed under a written binding contract." [KPMG TaxNewsFlash on Notice 2026-11](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/01/notice-2026-11-interim-guidance-100-percent-depreciation-deduction.html) (checked 2026-10-22); notice PDF at [irs.gov n-26-11](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-11.pdf) (link from KPMG; not opened)
- BDO summary: "The OBBBA permanently restores default 100% bonus depreciation for tangible MACRS property with a class life of 20 years or less, computer software, qualified improvement property, and certain other property listed in Section 168(k) that is acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025." Used property qualifies if "The property must not have been used by the taxpayer or a predecessor prior to its acquisition", it is acquired by purchase from an unrelated party, and basis is not carried over. Also: "Property must be both acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, to be eligible for 100% bonus depreciation." [BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/tax/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-expands-100-depreciation-expensing-opportunities) (checked 2026-10-22)
- 5-year property: IRC 168(e)(3)(B): "The term '5-year property' includes— ... (iv) any qualified technological equipment". IRC 168(i)(2)(A): "The term 'qualified technological equipment' means— (i) any computer or peripheral equipment". IRC 168(i)(2)(B)(ii): "The term 'computer' means a programmable electronically activated device which— (I) is capable of accepting information, applying prescribed processes to the information, and supplying the results of these processes with or without human intervention, and (II) consists of a central processing unit containing extensive storage, logic, arithmetic, and control capabilities." [26 USC 168](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 168(e)(1) classification table: "5-year property" is property with a class life of "More than 4 but less than 10". [26 USC 168](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Pub 946, Appendix B (Rev. Proc. 87-56 table), asset class 00.12: "00.12 Information Systems: Includes computers and their peripheral equipment used in administering normal business transactions and the maintenance of business records, their retrieval and analysis. Information systems are defined as: 1) Computers: A computer is a programmable electronically activated device capable of accepting information, applying prescribed processes to the information, and supplying the results of these processes with or without human intervention. It usually consists of a central processing unit containing extensive storage, logic, arithmetic, and control capabilities." The numeric columns of Table B-1 did not extract from the PDF text. [IRS Pub 946 PDF](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p946.pdf) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Pub 946 chapter 4, 5-year property list: "2. 5-year property. a. Automobiles, taxis, buses, helicopters, and trucks. ... c. Any qualified technological equipment." Pub 946 ADS table: "Computers and peripheral equipment .... 5 years". [IRS Pub 946 PDF](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p946.pdf) (checked 2026-10-22)
- FTB confirms the Rev. Proc. 87-56 class life: "Computers and peripheral equipment (printers, etc.): 6 yrs." presented as "the normal periods of useful life for the types of property listed as shown in IRS Rev. Proc. 87-56." [FTB 3885 instructions (2025)](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-3885-instructions.html) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Thomson Reuters MACRS Asset Life table (derived from Rev. Proc. 87-56) lists 00.12 Information Systems and notes qualified technological equipment in that class "is assigned a recovery period of 5 years notwithstanding its class life." [Thomson Reuters MACRS Asset Life table](https://thomsonreuters.com/en-us/help/fixed-assets-cs/screens/hid_help_asset_lives) (checked 2026-10-22, secondary)
- Placed in service, Pub 946: "You place property in service when it is ready and available for a specific use, whether in a business activity, an income-producing activity, a tax-exempt activity, or a personal activity. Even if you are not using the property, it is in service when it is ready and available for its specific use." Example 1: "You bought a machine for your business. The machine was delivered last year. However, it was not installed and operational until this year. It is considered placed in service this year. If the machine had been ready and available for use when it was delivered, it would be considered placed in service last year even if it was not actually used until this year." [IRS Pub 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.46-3(d)(1)(ii) (the regulatory source of the definition): property is placed in service in "The taxable year in which the property is placed in a condition or state of readiness and availability for a specifically assigned function, whether in a trade or business, in the production of income, in a tax-exempt activity, or in a personal activity." Example (d)(2)(iii): "Equipment is acquired for a specifically assigned function and is operational but is undergoing testing to eliminate any defects." is treated as placed in service. [26 CFR 1.46-3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.46-3) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Mid-quarter convention, IRC 168(d)(3)(A): if "the aggregate bases of property to which this section applies placed in service during the last 3 months of the taxable year, exceed" "40 percent of the aggregate bases of property to which this section applies placed in service during such taxable year, the applicable convention for all property to which this section applies placed in service during such taxable year shall be the mid-quarter convention." [26 USC 168](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- How it applies: A server or GPU bought under a purchase order signed after January 19, 2025 and racked in a colo, powered, networked, and able to run inference by December 31, 2026 is placed in service in 2026. The full cost is deductible federally in 2026 under 168(k). A server delivered in December 2026 but not racked or powered until January 2027 is placed in service in 2027.
- Used GPUs bought from an unrelated seller qualify for bonus if the owner never used them before (168(k)(2)(E)(ii), BDO summary).
- Bonus is 100 percent whatever the convention, so the mid-quarter convention does not change the federal 2026 deduction. It matters for California (Q4 and Q11).
- The asset must be used in a trade or business that has begun (Q9). If the business has not begun in 2026, the server is not yet "used in" a trade or business and the 2026 bonus claim is weak. Estimate: this is an inference from the placed-in-service definition ("in a trade or business"); the CPA should confirm the start date.

### Gaps
- Rev. Proc. 87-56 itself (1987-2 C.B. 674) was not retrieved; Pub 946 Appendix B reproduces its asset class 00.12 text, and FTB and Thomson Reuters state the 6-year class life. The numeric columns of Pub 946 Table B-1 did not extract from the PDF.
- Notice 2026-11 was cited through KPMG; the notice PDF was not opened.

## Q4. California non-conformity: no bonus depreciation; Section 179 capped at $25,000 with a $200,000 phase-out (R&TC 17255)

### Takeaway
California personal income tax law does not allow IRC 168(k) bonus depreciation and caps the Section 179 election at $25,000, reduced dollar for dollar once Section 179 property placed in service exceeds $200,000. A $100,000 server bought and placed in service in December 2026 yields a California year-one deduction of $25,000 (179) plus 5-year MACRS depreciation on the remaining $75,000: $15,000 under the half-year convention or $3,750 under the mid-quarter convention that applies when all property is placed in service in the fourth quarter.

### Cited Findings
- R&TC 17255(a): "Section 179(b)(1) of the Internal Revenue Code, relating to dollar limitation, shall not apply and in lieu thereof, the aggregate cost which may be taken into account under Section 179(a) of the Internal Revenue Code for any taxable year shall not exceed twenty-five thousand dollars ($25,000)." 17255(b): "the limitation under subdivision (a) for any taxable year shall be reduced, but not to below zero, by the amount by which the cost of Section 179 property ... placed in service during the taxable year exceeds two hundred thousand dollars ($200,000)." [R&TC 17255 (california.public.law)](https://california.public.law/codes/revenue_and_taxation_code_section_17255) (checked 2026-10-22); official source [leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=RTC&sectionNum=17255.)
- FTB Form 3885A instructions (individuals, 2025): the list of federal/state differences includes "Additional depreciation (IRC Section 168(k))", "Asset expense election (IRC Section 179)", and "Start-up expenses (IRC Section 195)". Part II: "Federal limitation amounts may be different than California limitation amounts. For California purposes, the maximum IRC Section 179 expense deduction allowed is $25,000." The worksheet line 3 reads "Threshold cost of IRC Section 179 property before reduction in limitation: $200,000". [FTB 3885A instructions (2025)](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-3885a-instructions.html) (checked 2026-10-22)
- FTB 3885A instructions: "If you elect this deduction, you must reduce your California depreciable basis by the IRC Section 179 expense." Line 12 limits the deduction to "the smaller of business income (not less than zero) or line 5" and line 13 carries over the disallowed amount. [FTB 3885A instructions (2025)](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-3885a-instructions.html) (checked 2026-10-22)
- FTB Form 3885 instructions (corporations, 2025) list under non-conformity "IRC Section 168(k) relating to the depreciation deduction for certain assets" and "The enhanced IRC Section 179 expensing election." The corporate instructions also say California does not conform to MACRS "for corporations"; that corporate rule does not govern a sole proprietor. [FTB 3885 instructions (2025)](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-3885-instructions.html) (checked 2026-10-22)
- The 2026 edition of the FTB instructions is not yet posted (the 2025 edition is the latest, checked 2026-10-22). FTB Pub 1001 (Supplemental Guidelines to California Adjustments) is referenced by both instruction sets as the detailed source. [FTB forms search](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/search/) (checked 2026-10-22)
- MACRS first-year rates, Pub 946 Table A-1 (half-year convention), 5-year property, year 1: "20.00%". Table A-5 (mid-quarter convention, placed in service in fourth quarter), 5-year property, year 1: "5.00%". [IRS Pub 946 PDF](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p946.pdf) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- California depreciation of a $100,000 server in year one (estimate):
  - Section 179 (California): $25,000, because total Section 179 property placed in service ($100,000) is under the $200,000 phase-out threshold.
  - Remaining California basis: $75,000.
  - MACRS 5-year, half-year convention: 20 percent of $75,000 = $15,000. California year-one total: $40,000.
  - MACRS 5-year, mid-quarter convention (applies if the server is the only MACRS property placed in service in 2026 and it goes into service in October to December, since 100 percent of basis is then in the last quarter, exceeding 40 percent): 5 percent of $75,000 = $3,750. California year-one total: $28,750.
  - A December 2026 in-service date therefore gives the smaller California figure. Placing the server in service by September 30, 2026 avoids the mid-quarter convention, but the business must also have begun by then (Q9).
- The California Section 179 deduction is limited to business income on the FTB worksheet. Under federal Reg. 1.179-2(c)(6)(iv), an individual's W-2 wages count as active trade or business income for the Section 179 income limit; this regulation was not retrieved in this research, so the CPA should confirm that California follows it for the $25,000 cap. Estimate.
- California PIT conformity to MACRS for a sole proprietor rests on R&TC 17250 (conformity to IRC 168 with modifications); that section was not scraped. The FTB 3885A instructions do not list MACRS as a difference for individuals, which supports conformity.

### Gaps
- R&TC 17250 text and FTB Pub 1001 text were not retrieved; the 3885A instructions are cited instead.

## Q5. Excess business loss limitation, IRC 461(l): 2026 threshold; NOL carryforward; California version

## Q6. Only a net business loss offsets W-2 wages; at-risk (IRC 465) and passive activity (IRC 469) rules; the seven material participation tests

### Takeaway
A Schedule C net loss flows to Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 3 and offsets wages, provided the owner is at risk for the money invested (box 32a) and materially participates (line G). Cash the owner contributes is at risk under 465(b)(1)(A). A sole proprietor who does the work himself meets material participation under the 500-hour test, the "substantially all" test, or the 100-hour-and-not-less-than-anyone-else test. Hours may be proven by "any reasonable means," but a contemporaneous log is the safest record.

### Cited Findings
- Schedule C instructions, Line 31: "Enter your net profit or loss on line 31 and include it on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 3." and "Also, include your net profit or loss on Schedule SE (Form 1040), line 2." For a loss, "check box 32a or 32b to indicate whether the loss from your business activity is limited by the at-risk rules." Line 32: "If all amounts are at risk in this business, check box 32a." "If some investment is not at risk, check box 32b; the at-risk rules apply to your loss." "Be sure to attach Form 6198 to your return." If you answered "No" on line G (material participation), "the passive activity loss rules may apply" and you may need to "complete Form 8582 to figure the passive activity loss to enter on line 31." [IRS Schedule C instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 465(a)(1): a loss from an activity "shall be allowed only to the extent of the aggregate amount with respect to which the taxpayer is at risk (within the meaning of subsection (b)) for such activity at the close of the taxable year." 465(b)(1): "a taxpayer shall be considered at risk for an activity with respect to amounts including— (A) the amount of money and the adjusted basis of other property contributed by the taxpayer to the activity, and (B) amounts borrowed with respect to such activity" to the extent the taxpayer "is personally liable for the repayment of such amounts". 465(b)(4): not at risk for "amounts protected against loss through nonrecourse financing, guarantees, stop loss agreements, or other similar arrangements." [26 USC 465](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/465) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 469(c)(1): "The term 'passive activity' means any activity— (A) which involves the conduct of any trade or business, and (B) in which the taxpayer does not materially participate." 469(h)(1): "A taxpayer shall be treated as materially participating in an activity only if the taxpayer is involved in the operations of the activity on a basis which is— (A) regular, (B) continuous, and (C) substantial." [26 USC 469](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/469) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.469-5T(a), the seven tests: an individual materially participates "if and only if— (1) The individual participates in the activity for more than 500 hours during such year; (2) The individual's participation in the activity for the taxable year constitutes substantially all of the participation in such activity of all individuals (including individuals who are not owners of interests in the activity) for such year; (3) The individual participates in the activity for more than 100 hours during the taxable year, and such individual's participation in the activity for the taxable year is not less than the participation in the activity of any other individual (including individuals who are not owners of interests in the activity) for such year; (4) The activity is a significant participation activity ... and the individual's aggregate participation in all significant participation activities during such year exceeds 500 hours; (5) The individual materially participated in the activity ... for any five taxable years (whether or not consecutive) during the ten taxable years that immediately precede the taxable year; (6) The activity is a personal service activity ... and the individual materially participated in the activity for any three taxable years ... preceding the taxable year; or (7) Based on all of the facts and circumstances ..., the individual participates in the activity on a regular, continuous, and substantial basis during such year." [26 CFR 1.469-5T](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.469-5T) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.469-5T(b)(2)(iii): "If an individual participates in an activity for 100 hours or less during the taxable year, such individual shall not be treated as materially participating in such activity for the taxable year under paragraph (a)(7)". [26 CFR 1.469-5T](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.469-5T) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii)(A): "Work done by an individual in the individual's capacity as an investor in an activity shall not be treated as participation in the activity for purposes of this section unless the individual is directly involved in the day-to-day management or operations of the activity." [26 CFR 1.469-5T](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.469-5T) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.469-5T(f)(3): a spouse's participation counts as the taxpayer's participation. [26 CFR 1.469-5T](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.469-5T) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Record-keeping, Reg. 1.469-5T(f)(4): "The extent of an individual's participation in an activity may be established by any reasonable means. Contemporaneous daily time reports, logs, or similar documents are not required if the extent of such participation may be established by other reasonable means. Reasonable means for purposes of this paragraph may include but are not limited to the identification of services performed over a period of time and the approximate number of hours spent performing such services during such period, based on appointment books, calendars, or narrative summaries." [26 CFR 1.469-5T](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.469-5T) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Ordering: IRC 461(l)(6) applies the excess business loss limit "after the application of section 469." [26 USC 461](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/461) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- How it applies: The owner funds the business with his own cash and has no outside investors or nonrecourse debt, so all amounts are at risk (box 32a). If he personally builds, operates, and markets the service, he will be the only participant, which satisfies test (2) "substantially all" even in a short first year, and likely tests (1) and (3) over a full year.
- A business that starts in December 2026 may have fewer than 100 hours in 2026. Test (2) does not have an hours floor, so a solo owner still materially participates in 2026 if no one else works more. If a contractor or vendor's staff do substantial work, test (3) (more than 100 hours and not less than anyone else) becomes the fallback, so keep hours above 100 or document that no other individual exceeded the owner's hours.
- Keep a simple weekly log (date, task, hours) from day one. The regulation does not require one, but it is the easiest "reasonable means."

### Gaps
- No case law on material participation for a solo online business was researched; the regulation text is clear enough for this fact pattern.

## Q7. Hobby loss, IRC 183: nine factors, 3-of-5-year presumption, business plans, and cases

### Takeaway
Section 183 denies losses from an activity "not engaged in for profit." The nine factors in Reg. 1.183-2(b) decide profit motive; a start-up period of losses is expected and is not fatal. A written, pre-existing business plan with a path to profit helps under factor (1) (businesslike manner) and factor (6) (losses explained by start-up stage); a plan produced after the audit begins hurts (Young). Huff (T.C. Memo. 2021-140) allowed years of start-up losses for a wealthy owner; Young (T.C. Memo. 2025-95) denied losses where the ranch never had books, the plan appeared after the audit, and the owners had large other income.

### Cited Findings
- IRC 183(a): "In the case of an activity engaged in by an individual or an S corporation, if such activity is not engaged in for profit, no deduction attributable to such activity shall be allowed under this chapter except as provided in this section." [26 USC 183](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/183) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 183(d), presumption: "If the gross income derived from an activity for 3 or more of the taxable years in the period of 5 consecutive taxable years which ends with the taxable year exceeds the deductions attributable to such activity ..., then, unless the Secretary establishes to the contrary, such activity shall be presumed for purposes of this chapter for such taxable year to be an activity engaged in for profit." 183(e) allows an election to defer the determination until "the close of the fourth taxable year ... following the taxable year in which the taxpayer first engages in the activity." [26 USC 183](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/183) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.183-2(a): "Although a reasonable expectation of profit is not required, the facts and circumstances must indicate that the taxpayer entered into the activity, or continued the activity, with the objective of making a profit." and "greater weight is given to objective facts than to the taxpayer's mere statement of his intent." [26 CFR 1.183-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.183-2) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.183-2(b), the nine factors (headings quoted): "(1) Manner in which the taxpayer carries on the activity." "(2) The expertise of the taxpayer or his advisors." "(3) The time and effort expended by the taxpayer in carrying on the activity." "(4) Expectation that assets used in activity may appreciate in value." "(5) The success of the taxpayer in carrying on other similar or dissimilar activities." "(6) The taxpayer's history of income or losses with respect to the activity." "(7) The amount of occasional profits, if any, which are earned." Factors (8) "The financial status of the taxpayer" and (9) "Elements of personal pleasure or recreation" were truncated in the scrape but are listed in the Journal of Accountancy summary of Young. "No one factor is determinative". [26 CFR 1.183-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.183-2) (checked 2026-10-22); [Journal of Accountancy, March 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2026/mar/court-determines-taxpayer-lacked-profit-motive/) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Factor (1) text: "The fact that the taxpayer carries on the activity in a businesslike manner and maintains complete and accurate books and records may indicate that the activity is engaged in for profit. ... A change of operating methods, adoption of new techniques or abandonment of unprofitable methods in a manner consistent with an intent to improve profitability may also indicate a profit motive." [26 CFR 1.183-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.183-2) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Factor (6) text: "A series of losses during the initial or start-up stage of an activity may not necessarily be an indication that the activity is not engaged in for profit. However, where losses continue to be sustained beyond the period which customarily is necessary to bring the operation to profitable status such continued losses, if not explainable, as due to customary business risks or reverses, may be indicative that the activity is not being engaged in for profit." [26 CFR 1.183-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.183-2) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Case allowed: Huff v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2021-140 (miniature donkey breeding, 2013-2014; losses every year 2010-2017; owner was a wealthy investment manager). "The Tax Court held that the Huffs' losses from Ecotone's donkey-breeding activity were allowable because they engaged in the activity with an actual and honest objective of making a profit." On factor (1): "Huff had a business plan for Ecotone, albeit unwritten, and when confronted with problems with its breeding operations, made changes that were intended to improve the activity's profitability." Ecotone "maintained books and records detailing donkey purchases and sales", "had its own bank account and credit card, and it maintained business filings as a separate entity." [The Tax Adviser, March 2022](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2022/mar/prior-business-success-credence-donkey-breeders-profit-motive/) (checked 2026-10-22). The Journal of Accountancy adds that the court said "a series of losses will not necessarily be held against a taxpayer if the activity is in its startup stage" and the activity "was still in its startup stage because it had been in operation for less than five years." [Journal of Accountancy, April 2022](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2022/apr/losses-millionaires-donkey-breeding-allowed.html) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Case denied: Young v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2025-95 (ranch, 2013-2014). The owners "managed the ranch informally, with no general ledger or formal bookkeeping." They reported "more than $2.9 million in losses from the ranch, while reporting passthrough income from ETI totaling nearly $16 million." A business plan was produced during audit, but "no evidence was established to prove that such a plan existed during 2013 and 2014. The IRS therefore concluded that the plan had been created after the audit had begun". Six of nine factors favored the IRS, and the court "sustained the IRS's disallowance of the Youngs' loss deductions". [Journal of Accountancy, March 2026](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2026/mar/court-determines-taxpayer-lacked-profit-motive/) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- How it applies: A GPU inference business has no recreational element (factor 9) and sells a service at market prices, so factors (1), (2), (3), and (6) carry the case. Write the business plan before year end 2026, date it, keep separate bank and card accounts, keep a ledger, track unit economics (cost per million tokens versus price), and record changes made to improve margins. The owner's high W-2 income (factor 8) is the main fact that invites scrutiny, as in Young.
- A written plan is not required (Huff's plan was unwritten) but an after-the-fact plan was treated as evidence against the taxpayer in Young.
- The 183(d) presumption needs profits in 3 of 5 years. A first-year loss followed by profits in 2027-2029 would secure the presumption by 2029.

### Gaps
- The Tax Court opinions themselves were cited through The Tax Adviser and the Journal of Accountancy, not read directly.
- The assignment asked for a case denying a "tax-motivated scheme"; Young is a hobby-loss denial where large other income and a post-audit plan were central, not a formal tax-shelter case. No section 183 case with an explicit tax-shelter finding was researched.

## Q8. Economic substance, IRC 7701(o), and the 20 or 40 percent penalty, IRC 6662(b)(6) and 6662(i)

### Takeaway
A transaction has economic substance only if it changes the taxpayer's economic position in a meaningful way apart from tax effects and the taxpayer has a substantial non-tax purpose. A business that buys or rents GPUs and sells inference at market prices changes its economic position (real costs, real revenue, real risk of loss) and has a substantial purpose (profit from selling compute), so it passes both prongs even in a loss year. The 20 percent penalty rises to 40 percent for undisclosed transactions that lack economic substance.

### Cited Findings
- IRC 7701(o)(1): "In the case of any transaction to which the economic substance doctrine is relevant, such transaction shall be treated as having economic substance only if— (A) the transaction changes in a meaningful way (apart from Federal income tax effects) the taxpayer's economic position, and (B) the taxpayer has a substantial purpose (apart from Federal income tax effects) for entering into such transaction." [26 USC 7701](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 7701(o)(2)(A): profit potential counts "only if the present value of the reasonably expected pre-tax profit from the transaction is substantial in relation to the present value of the expected net tax benefits that would be allowed if the transaction were respected." [26 USC 7701](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 7701(o)(3): "any State or local income tax effect which is related to a Federal income tax effect shall be treated in the same manner as a Federal income tax effect." [26 USC 7701](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 7701(o)(5)(B): "In the case of an individual, paragraph (1) shall apply only to transactions entered into in connection with a trade or business or an activity engaged in for the production of income." (5)(C): "The determination of whether the economic substance doctrine is relevant to a transaction shall be made in the same manner as if this subsection had never been enacted." [26 USC 7701](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 6662(a): the accuracy-related penalty is "20 percent of the portion of the underpayment to which this section applies." 6662(b)(6) applies it to "Any disallowance of claimed tax benefits by reason of a transaction lacking economic substance (within the meaning of section 7701(o)) or failing to meet the requirements of any similar rule of law." [26 USC 6662](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 6662(i)(1): "In the case of any portion of an underpayment which is attributable to one or more nondisclosed noneconomic substance transactions, subsection (a) shall be applied with respect to such portion by substituting '40 percent' for '20 percent'." 6662(i)(2): a nondisclosed transaction is one "with respect to which the relevant facts affecting the tax treatment are not adequately disclosed in the return nor in a statement attached to the return." [26 USC 6662](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- How it applies: Renting GPUs from an unrelated cloud and selling tokens to unrelated customers at posted prices is an ordinary business operation, not a structured transaction. Prong (A) is met because the owner spends real money and bears real risk; prong (B) is met because the purpose is to earn revenue. Year-one losses do not change this. The reasonably expected pre-tax profit over the plan horizon should be documented (the 7701(o)(2) profit-potential test) because the first-year tax benefit from a loss is large relative to first-year pre-tax profit.
- Risk rises if payments go to related parties, if prices are not at market, if the "customer" is the owner himself or his employer, or if the compute is never actually used. Avoid circular arrangements.
- Disclosure removes the 40 percent tier. Reporting the loss on Schedule C with full books is normal disclosure; a Form 8275 is not needed for ordinary business expenses.

### Gaps
- No IRS guidance applying 7701(o) to ordinary operating businesses was retrieved (for example the 2011 LB&I directive on when to raise the doctrine). The statute text is relied on.

## Q9. Start-up costs, IRC 195: $5,000 first-year deduction, $50,000 phase-out, 180-month amortization, and when a business "begins"

### Takeaway
Costs paid before the active trade or business begins are start-up expenditures: up to $5,000 is deductible in the year the business begins (phased out dollar for dollar above $50,000 of start-up costs), and the rest is amortized over 180 months starting with the month the business begins. Under Kellett, an online business begins when it is open to the public and able to take customers, even if the first revenue comes later. If the API is live and open for paid signups in December 2026, December costs are section 162 expenses; if it is not open until January 2027, all 2026 spending is capitalized under 195 and nothing is deductible in 2026.

### Cited Findings
- IRC 195(a): "Except as otherwise provided in this section, no deduction shall be allowed for start-up expenditures." 195(b)(1)(A): a deduction "for the taxable year in which the active trade or business begins in an amount equal to the lesser of— (i) the amount of start-up expenditures with respect to the active trade or business, or (ii) $5,000, reduced (but not below zero) by the amount by which such start-up expenditures exceed $50,000". 195(b)(1)(B): "the remainder of such start-up expenditures shall be allowed as a deduction ratably over the 180-month period beginning with the month in which the active trade or business begins." [26 USC 195](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/195) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRC 195(c)(1): start-up expenditures are amounts "paid or incurred in connection with— (i) investigating the creation or acquisition of an active trade or business, or (ii) creating an active trade or business, or (iii) any activity engaged in for profit and for the production of income before the day on which the active trade or business begins, in anticipation of such activity becoming an active trade or business," and which "would be allowable as a deduction for the taxable year in which paid or incurred" if the business already existed. 195(c)(2)(A): "the determination of when an active trade or business begins shall be made in accordance with such regulations as the Secretary may prescribe." (No such regulation defines the start date.) [26 USC 195](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/195) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Reg. 1.195-1(b): "A taxpayer is deemed to have made an election under section 195(b) to amortize start-up expenditures ... for the taxable year in which the active trade or business to which the expenditures relate begins." Example 2: a business that begins July 1, 2011 with $41,000 of start-up costs "may deduct $5,000 and the portion of the remaining $36,000 that is allocable to July through December of 2011 ($36,000/180 × 6 = $1,200) in 2011". Example 6: with $450,000 of start-up costs, only the monthly amortization ($15,000 for six months) is deductible in the first year. [26 CFR 1.195-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.195-1) (checked 2026-10-22)
- IRS Pub 583: "Business start-up costs are the expenses you incur before you actually begin business operations." "You can elect to deduct up to $5,000 of business start-up costs and up to $5,000 of organizational costs." The $5,000 "is reduced by the amount your start-up or organizational costs exceed $50,000." "Any remaining costs must be amortized." [IRS Pub 583](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Kellett v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2022-62: the taxpayer built a financial website from 2013, "finally opened the desktop and mobile applications to the public on Sept. 30, 2015," and "In 2019, the taxpayer started to earn revenue from the business." The IRS argued the business was not active until 2019. "However, the court determined the business likely became active in September 2015, when the website was open to the public." The court relied on Richmond Television Corp. v. United States, 345 F.2d 901 (4th Cir. 1965) ("a business did not begin until the television station went on the air") and quoted Cabintaxi Corp. v. Commissioner, 63 F.3d 614 (7th Cir. 1995): "a taxpayer's effort to sell goods or services may qualify as an active trade or business even if the taxpayer makes no sales and therefore has zero gross receipts." Holding: "expenses paid prior to Sept. 30, 2015, are capitalizable under Section 195, while those paid after that date are deductible under Section 162." [Grant Thornton, July 2022](https://www.grantthornton.com/library/newsletters/tax/2022/hot-topics/jul-05/tax-court-rules-on-online-business-start-up-expenses.aspx) (checked 2026-10-22)
- The commonly cited test from Richmond Television is that a business begins once it "has begun to function as a going concern and performed those activities for which it was organized." [Freeman Law summary of Kellett](https://freemanlaw.com/tax-court-in-brief-kellett-v-commissioner-start-up-research-expenses-and-other-itemized-business-deductions/) (checked 2026-10-22, secondary)
- California conforms: FTB states "California law conforms to the federal law which allows a deduction for business start-up and organizational costs paid or incurred during a taxable year." [FTB 3885 instructions (2025)](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-3885-instructions.html) (checked 2026-10-22)

### Inferences
- How it applies, first paying customer in December 2026: the business began in 2026 (revenue is the strongest evidence of a going concern). - First paying customer in January 2027, but the API was publicly open and accepting signups in December 2026: Kellett supports a December 2026 start. - The depreciation of an owned server also depends on the business having begun; see Q3 inference.

## Q11. Checking the owner's arithmetic: $100,000 expense saves $49,300; $100,000 owned server saves about $40,500

### Takeaway
At a 37 percent federal marginal rate and 12.3 percent California rate, a $100,000 deductible expense saves $49,300, which matches the owner's figure. A $100,000 server saves $37,000 federally (100 percent bonus) plus California tax on $25,000 of Section 179 and MACRS on the remaining $75,000: $40,536 under the mid-quarter convention (December in-service date) or $41,920 under the half-year convention. The owner's "about $40,500" matches the mid-quarter case. No NIIT applies to a non-passive business loss or profit, and no SE tax arises from a loss. All figures are estimates that ignore the SALT cap, the deductibility of state tax, the 1 percent California mental health tax above $1 million, AMT, and the 461(l) cap.

### Cited Findings
- Rates: 37 percent is the top federal bracket under IRC 1(j); the 2026 rate tables are in Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.01 ("Tax Rate Tables ... 1(j)(2)(A)-(D)"). [Rev. Proc. 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf) (checked 2026-10-22; the bracket dollar amounts were not extracted)
- California top rate: the 2025 Schedule X (single) reaches "12.30%" on taxable income over $742,953 and Schedule Y (joint) reaches 12.30% over $1,485,906. [FTB 2025 Tax Rate Schedules](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-540-tax-rate-schedules.pdf) (checked 2026-10-22). The 1 percent Mental Health Services Tax on taxable income over $1,000,000 (R&TC 17043) was not retrieved; it would raise the California rate to 13.3 percent for owners above that level.
- Federal bonus: 100 percent of basis in the year placed in service (Q3). California: $25,000 Section 179 cap and no bonus (Q4). MACRS year-one rates 20 percent (half-year) and 5 percent (fourth-quarter mid-quarter) (Q4).
- NIIT: 3.8 percent applies to net investment income, which excludes income from a trade or business that is not a passive activity (IRC 1411(c)(1)(A)(i) and (c)(2)) and excludes self-employment income (1411(c)(6)). [26 USC 1411](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1411) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Excess business loss cap for 2026: $256,000 single, $512,000 joint (Q5).

### Inferences
- Expense case (estimate): $100,000 × 37% = $37,000 federal; $100,000 × 12.3% = $12,300 California; total $49,300. Confirmed.
- Owned server, December 2026 in-service, mid-quarter convention (estimate): federal $100,000 × 37% = $37,000. California deduction = $25,000 (179) + 5% × $75,000 ($3,750) = $28,750; $28,750 × 12.3% = $3,536. Total $40,536, about $40,500. Confirmed on the mid-quarter assumption.
- Owned server, half-year convention (in service by September 30, 2026, or other property placed in service earlier in the year so that fourth-quarter basis is 40 percent or less) (estimate): California deduction = $25,000 + 20% × $75,000 ($15,000) = $40,000; $40,000 × 12.3% = $4,920. Total $41,920.
- The California shortfall is timing, not a permanent loss: the remaining $71,250 (mid-quarter) or $60,000 (half-year) of California basis is depreciated in 2027 through 2031 (and a partial year 2032 under mid-quarter).
- Other effects: (a) A loss creates no SE tax and no NIIT; NIIT on the owner's other investment income is unaffected because a non-passive business loss is not "properly allocable" to net investment income. (b) If total 2026 business deductions exceed business income by more than $256,000 (single), the excess is deferred to an NOL (Q5), so the marginal saving on dollars above the cap is zero in 2026. (c) State tax deductibility is capped by the federal SALT limit, which the owner chose to ignore; the federal saving from a California deduction is therefore not reduced here. (d) AMT and the California AMT were not analyzed. (e) A 37 percent marginal rate assumes 2026 taxable income above the top bracket threshold; the 2026 thresholds were not extracted.

### Gaps
- Federal 2026 bracket thresholds, R&TC 17043 (mental health tax), IRC 164(f), and the AMT interaction were not retrieved.

## Part 3. Data privacy, logging, and export controls for an inference provider

### Takeaway
OpenRouter requires listed providers to publish a privacy policy with "clear data retention terms" and to "disclose whether prompts are logged and if data is used for training," and it surfaces those policies per endpoint. A sole proprietor below $26,625,000 in annual gross revenue who does not buy, sell, or share personal information of 100,000 or more California consumers is not a "business" under the CCPA, but California's CalOPPA still requires a conspicuously posted privacy policy for any commercial website or online service that collects personal information from California consumers, and the model licenses require a terms of service. No export control was found that restricts providing inference as a service of open-weight models to customers in non-embargoed countries; the January 2025 AI Diffusion Rule's model-weight control excluded open-weight models and BIS announced its rescission in May 2025.

### Cited Findings
- OpenRouter provider application page, Technical requirements: "Privacy & Data Policy: Have a published privacy policy and clear data retention terms. Providers must disclose whether prompts are logged and if data is used for training." Also: "Your /chat/completions endpoint must be OpenAI-compatible, return usage tokens for both stream and non-stream requests, and support streaming." "Providers maintaining 95%+ uptime retain standard routing priority." "We currently have a large backlog of provider applications and are prioritizing providers with proprietary models." "Declare your datacenter locations in the /models endpoint." [OpenRouter, Become a Provider](https://openrouter.ai/providers/apply) (checked 2026-10-22)
- OpenRouter provider logging doc: "Each provider on OpenRouter has its own data handling policies. We reflect those policies in structured data on each AI endpoint that we offer." "Wherever possible, OpenRouter works with providers to ensure that prompts will not be trained on, but there are exceptions. If you opt out of training in your account settings, OpenRouter will not route to providers that train." "Providers also have their own data retention policies, often for compliance reasons. OpenRouter does not have routing rules that change based on data retention policies of providers, but the retention policies as reflected in each provider's terms are shown". [OpenRouter Provider Logging](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/privacy/provider-logging) (checked 2026-10-22)
- CCPA applicability (California AG): "The CCPA applies to for-profit businesses that do business in California and meet any of the following: Have a gross annual revenue of over $25 million; Buy, sell, or share the personal information of 100,000 or more California residents or households; or Derive 50% or more of their annual revenue from selling California residents' personal information." [California AG, CCPA](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) (checked 2026-10-22)
- CPI adjustment: the California Privacy Protection Agency announced the Civil Code 1798.140(d)(1)(A) revenue threshold is "$26,625,000, up from $25,000,000" effective January 1, 2025, adjusted every odd-numbered year. [CPPA announcement, Dec 17, 2024](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20241217.html) (checked 2026-10-22). The next adjustment is due January 1, 2027.
- B2B data: "Are the statutory exemptions for employee data and business-to-business transactions still in effect? No. The exemptions for employment-related personal information and personal information reflecting business-to-business transactions described in Civil Code Sec. 1798.145(m)-(n) expired on December 31, 2022." [California AG, CCPA](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) (checked 2026-10-22)
- CCPA covers only natural persons: "A California resident is a natural person (as opposed to a corporation or other business entity) who resides in California". [California AG, CCPA](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Privacy policy requirement (CalOPPA), Bus. & Prof. Code 22575(a): "An operator of a commercial Web site or online service that collects personally identifiable information through the Internet about individual consumers residing in California who use or visit its commercial Web site or online service shall conspicuously post its privacy policy on its Web site". 22575(b) requires the policy to identify the categories of PII collected and third parties it is shared with, describe the process for notifying consumers of material changes, state its effective date, and disclose how the operator responds to "do not track" signals. [B&P 22575 (california.public.law)](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_22575) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Terms of service: the Gemma Terms (3.1) and the DeepSeek-V3 original model license (4.a) require the provider to bind its users to the upstream use restrictions through "any agreement (e.g., license agreement, terms of use, etc.)"; the Llama AUP says "You agree you will not use, or allow others to use, Llama 4 to" perform the prohibited uses. [Gemma Terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/terms), [DeepSeek LICENSE-MODEL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3/main/LICENSE-MODEL), [Llama 4 AUP](https://www.llama.com/llama4/use-policy/) (checked 2026-10-22)
- Export controls: the BIS "Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion" (interim final rule published January 2025, [Federal Register PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-15/pdf/2025-00636.pdf), not opened) "added a control for advanced AI model weights" (ECCN 4E091) and "added new red flag guidance related to AI model weights". On May 13, 2025, "BIS announced its plan to rescind the Biden Administration's Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion and that, until the rule is formally revoked, it would not enforce its worldwide licensing and other requirements that required compliance on May 15, 2025." "BIS did not announce any plans to change the pre-existing controls over advanced computing items, which, for example, require licenses to ship such items to most countries in the Middle East, China, and companies worldwide if headquartered in or with a parent in China." BIS also issued a policy statement that "providing advanced computing items or services to infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) providers (e.g., data centers), wherever located, to train AI models for or on behalf of entities headquartered in China or other arms embargoed countries creates a red flag". [Akin Gump, May 21, 2025](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/bis-rescinds-its-ai-diffusion-rule-and-issues-compliance-guidance-regarding-advanced-computing-items) (checked 2026-10-22); BIS policy statement at [bis.gov](https://www.bis.gov/media/documents/ai-policy-statement-training-ai-models-may-13-2025) (link from Akin; not opened)
- The model-weight control did not reach open weights: "BIS has not extended this licensing requirement to model weights of open-weight AI models, reflecting the U.S. government's present assessment of the balance of risks and benefits of such models." (search summary quoting the rule). [Mondaq summary](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/new-technology/1572522/bis-issues-new-rule-expanding-controls-on-export-reexport-and-transfer-of-advanced-computing-chips-and-artificial-intelligence-model-weights) (checked 2026-10-22, secondary)
- Status in 2026 (search summaries, secondary): a Miller & Chevalier mid-2026 update says the AI Diffusion Rule "has yet to be formally rescinded via an update to the Code of Federal Regulations" and BIS has taken a non-enforcement approach; no successor rule has been published. [Miller & Chevalier, 2025 year review and 2026 mid-year update](https://www.millerchevalier.com/publication/bis-export-controls-2025-year-review-and-2026-mid-year-update) (checked 2026-10-22, secondary, not opened)

### Inferences
- How it applies: Write and post (1) a privacy policy that states what is logged (request metadata, prompts, completions), how long it is kept, whether anything is used for training (say no), where data is processed (datacenter location), and how to request deletion; and (2) a terms of service that passes through the Llama AUP, Gemma Prohibited Use Policy, and any other upstream restrictions, and that requires customers to obtain consents for personal data they send. These documents are required by OpenRouter to list, by CalOPPA for any California visitor, and by the model licenses.
- The CCPA's "business" thresholds will not be met by a sole proprietor with modest revenue unless the service collects personal information of 100,000 or more California consumers or households (API customers are mostly businesses, but their end users' prompts may contain personal information; keep logging minimal). The B2B exemption is gone, so if thresholds are ever met, business contacts' data is covered.
- Export controls: serving an API of open-weight models to customers in non-embargoed countries requires no BIS license under the sources found. Transferring the weights themselves is not controlled for open-weight models. Do not sell or export the GPUs, and do not provide training capacity to entities headquartered in China or other arms-embargoed countries (BIS red flag). OFAC sanctions (Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Syria, and certain Russian and Ukrainian regions) still bar serving sanctioned persons; this was not researched here.

### Gaps
- OpenRouter's provider terms of service (the contract itself) were not retrieved; only the public application page and docs were.
- The BIS Federal Register text, the May 13, 2025 policy statement, and any 2026 BIS action were not opened; status rests on law-firm summaries. ECCN 4E091's current status in the CFR was not verified.
- OFAC sanctions screening obligations for an online service were not researched.
- The Commerce Department's 2024 proposed IaaS customer identification ("know your customer") rule for US cloud providers was not researched.
