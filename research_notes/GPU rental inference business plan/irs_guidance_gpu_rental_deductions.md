# IRS, Treasury, Tax Court and practitioner guidance on deducting GPU rental and GPU hardware

Scope: a US cash-basis sole proprietor (Schedule C) who rents cloud GPUs, and may buy GPU hardware, to sell AI inference to customers. Research date: 2026-10-22. Read-only research, not legal or tax advice. Quotes are verbatim and under 200 characters. "Estimate" marks my own inference. "Not found" marks items searched for that do not appear to exist in public sources.

## Is it legal to write off, and what has the IRS said

Yes. Renting GPUs to serve paying customers is an ordinary and necessary expense of a trade or business under IRC 162(a), and the hardware is 5-year property that qualifies for 100% bonus depreciation if acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. No IRS ruling, notice, CCA, PLR, or audit guide says cloud compute is not deductible, and none says it specially is; the IRS has never issued cloud-specific 162 guidance (not found). What the IRS has said is general: Pub 334 says rent for property you do not own is deductible, the Schedule C instructions put rented "machinery, or equipment" on line 20a and list "Computing infrastructure providers" as a business code, Treasury's 2025 final regulations classify every "cloud transaction" as "the provision of services" (for international provisions only), and Notice 2023-63 recognizes software "accessed remotely ... via cloud computing" and treats "Rent, utilities" on equipment used in development as research costs. The real legal risk is not the character of the expense. It is the loss rules: IRC 183 (profit motive, with an IRS audit guide that flags "Minimal income and large expenses for several years and those losses are offsetting other income"), IRC 461(l) ($256,000 single / $512,000 joint cap on business losses against wages in 2026), IRC 469 (you must materially participate), IRC 465 (you must be at risk), and IRC 6662 penalties. A business with an honest profit objective can deduct losses in early years; a "tax play" with no realistic profit path cannot.

## 1. Cloud compute and GPU rental as a deductible expense

Takeaway: GPU rental used to serve customers is deductible under IRC 162(a) in the year paid, as rent (162(a)(3)) or as a purchased service; the label does not change the result for a cash-basis taxpayer. The IRS has not written anything cloud-specific about 162. The closest IRS statements are the Pub 334 rent rules, Schedule C line 20a, Treasury's 2025 cloud regulations (services), Notice 2023-63 (cloud-delivered software), and the research-credit "computer rental" rule in Reg. 1.41-2(b)(4).

Primary sources:

- IRC 162(a), https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/162 : "There shall be allowed as a deduction all the ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred during the taxable year" "in carrying on any trade or business". Paragraph (a)(3): "rentals or other payments required to be made as a condition to the continued use or possession, for purposes of the trade or business, of property" "to which the taxpayer has not taken or is not taking title or in which he has no equity."
- IRS Pub 334 (2025), chapter 8, https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334 : "To be deductible, a business expense must be both ordinary and necessary." "An ordinary expense is one that is common and accepted in your field of business." "A necessary expense is one that is helpful and appropriate for your business." "An expense does not have to be indispensable to be considered necessary." Rent: "Rent is any amount you pay for the use of property you do not own." "If you pay rent in advance, you can deduct only the amount that applies to your use of the rented property during the tax year." "You can’t take a rental deduction for unreasonable rents."
- Pub 334 on "online services": not found. The 2025 edition does not use the phrases "online services", "cloud", or "subscription" in chapter 8. Pub 535 (Business Expenses) was discontinued after the 2022 edition; Pub 334 chapter 8 is its successor for sole proprietors.
- IRS Tax Tip 2022-37 (March 9, 2022), https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/small-business-rent-expenses-may-be-tax-deductible : "Rent is any amount paid for the use of property that a small business doesn't own." "Typically, rent can be deducted as a business expense when the rent is for property the taxpayer uses for the business." "Businesses must first determine whether an agreement is a lease or a conditional sales contract."
- Instructions for Schedule C (2025), https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc : "Lines 20a and 20b If you rented or leased vehicles, machinery, or equipment, enter on line 20a the business portion of your rental cost." "Technology and software tools. You can deduct technology and software tools that are ordinary and necessary expenses directly related to operating your business." "Generally, this will include tax preparation software and subscription services paid to manage your business." Business code list: "518210 - Computing infrastructure providers, data processing, web hosting, & related services". What's New: "Certain qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, can take a 100% bonus depreciation."
- Treasury Decision 10022 (final regs on digital content and cloud transactions, Federal Register January 14, 2025), https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-14/html/2024-31372.htm : "the final regulations define a cloud transaction as a transaction through which a person obtains on-demand network access to computer hardware" "digital content (as defined in Sec. 1.861-18(a)(2)), or other similar resources." "The final regulations treat all cloud transactions solely as the provision of services." Heading: "Final Regulations Apply Only for Certain International Provisions of the Code." Treasury also said it "could not identify a transaction that satisfies the definition of a cloud transaction" that would be a lease. Estimate: this is the only place the IRS has defined "cloud transaction"; it does not govern 162, but it shows the IRS views on-demand GPU access as a purchased service, not a lease of equipment.
- Notice 2023-63, section 5.02(1), https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-23-63.pdf : software code "may be stored on a computing device, affixed to a tangible medium (for example, a disk or DVD), or accessed remotely via a private computer network or the Internet, for example, via cloud computing."
- Reg. 1.162-11(a), https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.162-11 : covers leasehold acquisition costs and taxes paid for a landlord. It has no cloud or equipment-rental language.
- Research credit "computer rental" rule, Reg. 1.41-2(b)(4), as described in The Tax Adviser (KPMG authors, June 30, 2026), https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2026/jun/rd-credits-and-the-computer-rental-rules-time-forgot/ : "The computer must be owned and operated by someone other than the taxpayer" "the computer must be located off the taxpayer's premises" "the taxpayer must not be the computer's primary user". "neither Sec. 41(b)(2)(A)(iii) nor Regs. Sec. 1.41-2(b)(4) has been revised" and "no proposed regulations have been issued". The authors note the only IRS mention of computer rental costs in the June 2025 Sec. 41 Audit Techniques Guide "is text from the statute itself."

Not found: any IRS publication, notice, revenue ruling, revenue procedure, CCA, PLR, or audit technique guide that discusses "cloud computing", "hosting", "SaaS", "IaaS", "computing capacity", "server rental", or "GPU rental" as a section 162 expense. The only IRS documents that use the word "cloud" are T.D. 10022 (international sourcing) and Notice 2023-63 (section 174 software definition).

## 2. Prepaid compute, reserved instances and cloud credits under the 12-month rule

Takeaway: Pay-as-you-go GPU bills are deductible when paid. A prepaid term of 12 months or less that ends by the end of the next tax year fits the 12-month rule in Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f) and is deductible when paid, if the payment is a real payment (not refundable) and the prepayment has a business reason such as a discount. A 3-year prepaid reservation must be capitalized and deducted ratably. Cloud "credits" that are refundable, have no fixed quantity, or can be redirected to any future service look like deposits under Keller and Rev. Rul. 79-229 and are deductible only as used. No IRS guidance addresses cloud credits or reserved instances (not found).

Primary sources:

- Reg. 1.461-1(a)(1), https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.461-1 : cash-method deductions "shall, as a general rule, be taken into account for the taxable year in which paid." But "If an expenditure results in the creation of an asset having a useful life which extends substantially beyond the close of the taxable year," "such an expenditure may not be deductible, or may be deductible only in part, for the taxable year in which made."
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(d)(3)(i), https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.263(a)-4 : "A taxpayer must capitalize prepaid expenses." Example 2 (prepaid rent): a cash-method taxpayer prepays $240,000 on a 24-month lease; the $240,000 "is a prepaid expense and must be capitalized under this paragraph (d)(3)."
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(1), the 12-month rule: "a taxpayer is not required to capitalize under this section amounts paid" to create a right or benefit that "does not extend beyond the earlier of" "12 months after the first date on which the taxpayer realizes the right or benefit; or" "The end of the taxable year following the taxable year in which the payment is made."
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(5), renewals: "the duration of a right includes any renewal period" if the facts show a reasonable expectancy of renewal. Estimate: an auto-renewing reservation can fail the rule if renewal is reasonably expected.
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(6), coordination with section 461: "In the case of a taxpayer using an accrual method of accounting, the rules of this paragraph (f) do not affect" "the determination of whether a liability is incurred during the taxable year". This limit is for accrual taxpayers; a cash-basis taxpayer uses 1.461-1(a)(1).
- Reg. 1.263(a)-4(f)(8) examples (numbering as displayed on the LII page). Prepaid rent, cash-method variant (Example 10): "U corporation leases office space from W corporation at a monthly rental rate of $2,000." "On August 1, 2005, U prepays its office rent expense for the first six months of 2006 in the amount of $12,000." "U is not required to capitalize its prepaid rent." Prepaid services, accrual (Example 11): "N corporation pays R corporation, an advertising and marketing firm, $40,000 on August 1, 2005," "Accordingly, because economic performance with respect to N's prepaid advertising expense does not occur until 2006," "Thus, the rules of this paragraph (f) do not apply to N's payment." Prepaid license (Example 5): "R's payment of each $500 annual fee is a prepaid expense described in paragraph (d)(3) of this section." "R is not required to capitalize the $500 fee in each taxable year."
- A 1.263(a)-4 example about a contract for a fixed quantity: not found. The "fixed quantity" test comes from the prepaid feed rulings, below.
- Rev. Rul. 79-229, 1979-2 C.B. 210. The ruling text is not available on a free public site (not found online). Its three-part test is quoted by the Eighth Circuit in Keller, https://openjurist.org/725/f2d/1173 : "the expenditure must be a payment ... rather than a mere deposit; second, the prepayment must be made for a business purpose and not merely for tax avoidance;" "and third, the deduction of such costs in the taxable year of prepayment must not result in a material distortion of income." The Ninth Circuit in Van Raden, https://openjurist.org/650/f2d/1046 , footnote 2: "Rev.Rul. 75-152 has been superseded by Rev.Rul. 79-229, 1979-2 Cum.Bull. 210. This later revenue ruling is not substantially different from the earlier one." The deposit factors as summarized by the University of Illinois Tax School (2010 workbook, secondary), https://resources.taxschool.illinois.edu/taxbookarchive/2010/2010%2010%20Ag%20Issues.pdf : "a. Absence of a specific quantity of items purchased" "b. A right to a refund of any credit that remains" "c. Treatment of the expenditures as a deposit on the seller's books" "d. A right to substitute other goods for those specified in the purchase contract".
- Zaninovich v. Commissioner, 616 F.2d 429 (9th Cir. 1980), https://openjurist.org/616/f2d/429 : cash-basis farmers paid a full year of rent on December 20 for a lease year ending the next November 30. "We hold that the payment in this case was fully deductible in the year of payment." "Under the 'one-year rule' an expenditure is treated as a capital expenditure if it creates an asset, or secures a like advantage to the taxpayer, having a useful life in excess of one year." "There was, however, no contention or evidence at trial that there was either a distortion of income or an attempt by the taxpayers here to evade taxes in some way."
- Bonaire Development Co. v. Commissioner, 679 F.2d 159 (9th Cir. 1982), https://openjurist.org/679/f2d/159 : prepaid management fees that were not yet due were not deductible. "If the taxpayer voluntarily makes an expenditure for future services or goods and the obligation is not due, the prepayment is not an ordinary and necessary business expense" of the period of payment, with exceptions. "Voluntary prepayments also may be deductible if the taxpayer has an appropriate or compelling business reason for making a payment in advance other than that it is due," "such as to take advantage of depressed prices or to secure future deliveries or preferential treatment". The court distinguished Zaninovich because there "Advance payment was required by the terms of the lease negotiated at arms length".
- Keller v. Commissioner, 725 F.2d 1173 (8th Cir. 1984), https://openjurist.org/725/f2d/1173 : "If a taxpayer retains unilateral power to regain the funds or to otherwise redirect their use, the transfer is deemed a 'deposit' rather than a payment," "Refundability is the crucial determination; if a taxpayer retains unilateral power to have the prepayment refunded, then the transfer is a nondeductible deposit." "Unless a business purpose requires a prepayment to be made in a certain year, the prepayment cannot fairly be characterized as an 'ordinary and necessary' business expense of that taxable year."
- Grynberg v. Commissioner, 83 T.C. 255 (1984). The opinion text is not on a free public site (not found online). The Tax Court restated its holding in Howe v. Commissioner (2000), https://law.onecle.com/tax/2000/howe.wpd.tcm.wpd05.html : "we have held that a cash-basis taxpayer may deduct prepaid items when paid, including rent" "if the taxpayer paid the rent (i.e., did not make a mere deposit)" "had a 'substantial business reason for making the prepayment in the year' it was made" and the prepayment did not "cause a material distortion in the taxpayer's taxable income in the year of prepayment." (citing Grynberg at 265-266).

IRS or practitioner commentary on prepaid SaaS, hosting, cloud credits, reserved instances:

- IRS guidance on prepaid cloud, SaaS, hosting, cloud credits, or reserved instances: not found.
- Aprio (Dave Carter-Hanson, CPA, July 29, 2026), https://www.aprio.com/insights-events/ai-spend-and-the-rd-credit-what-qualifies-and-when-ins-article-rd/ : "reserved instances, compute commitments, and savings plans do not change that character; they are prepaid ways to pay for the right to use computers". "Reserved capacity, compute commitments, and prepaid token bundles are paid up front but used over time." "For cash-method taxpayers, it generally falls when the amount is paid." (written in the research-credit context).
- Afternoon.co, "GPU Cloud Credits Accounting Treatment" (January 15, 2026, vendor blog), https://www.afternoon.co/blog/gpu-cloud-credit-accounting : "Cash basis taxpayers can often deduct prepaid GPU credits in the year of payment". For credits that run past 12 months: "you must capitalize the expense and deduct it as you use the credits." "Some GPU credits expire unused." It does not discuss the deposit question.
- Forbes, Tony Nitti, "When Can A Business Deduct Prepaid Expenses?" (May 25, 2015), https://www.forbes.com/sites/anthonynitti/2015/05/25/tax-geek-teusday-when-can-a-business-deduct-prepaid-expenses/ : "the 12-month rule does not trump any disallowance of a prepaid expense under a different area of the Code." "Courtesy of the 12-month rule, however, most prepayments by a cash basis taxpayer that satisfy the rule can be deducted immediately." On existing businesses: "you must apply for a change in accounting method on Form 3115 and receive the consent of the IRS; fortunately, the change related to deducting prepaids is an automatic change".
- Baker Newman Noyes (Adam Aucoin, November 1, 2015), https://www.bnncpa.com/resources/accelerating-your-deduction-for-prepaid-expenses/ : "simply prepaying an expense does not make it immediately deductible (barring an exception due to the 12-month rule)." Its example of a two-year "computer maintenance service program" "must be capitalized because it does not meet the 12-month rule".

Estimate on cloud credits: a nonrefundable credit bundle, bought at a discount, that expires within 12 months and can only be applied to the provider's compute is the strongest case for deduction when paid. Refundable credits, credits with no expiry, or credits the buyer can redirect to any product fit three of the four deposit factors and are safer deducted as consumed. Treat "committed use" contracts (a binding commitment to pay monthly for 1 or 3 years, not prepaid) as deductible month by month as paid.

## 3. Section 174 / 174A: development compute versus operating compute

Takeaway: After P.L. 119-21 (OBBBA, July 4, 2025), domestic research or experimental expenditures paid or incurred in tax years beginning after December 31, 2024 are deductible under new IRC 174A. Software development costs are research expenditures by statute (174A(d)(3)). GPU rental used to build and test an inference serving stack before it is placed in service is a software development cost and therefore a 174A expenditure; GPU rental used to serve paying customers is a 162 operating expense. For a cash-basis sole proprietor both are deductible in the year paid, so the split rarely changes the current-year number. It still matters for: foreign research (still 15-year amortization under 174), the optional 174A(c) amortization election, method-of-accounting consistency, and the research credit under 41 and 280C. Neither Rev. Proc. 2025-28 nor Notice 2023-63 mentions GPU rental or cloud compute as a cost item; Notice 2023-63 does list "Rent, utilities" on equipment used in development as research costs and excludes periodic "website hosting" fees.

Primary sources:

- IRC 174A, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/174A : (a) "Notwithstanding section 263, there shall be allowed as a deduction any domestic research or experimental expenditures." (b) the term means "research or experimental expenditures paid or incurred by the taxpayer in connection with the taxpayer's trade or business" other than those "attributable to foreign research." (d)(3) "any amount paid or incurred in connection with the development of any software shall be treated as a research or experimental expenditure." (d)(1) the section does not apply to "the acquisition or improvement of land" or depreciable property, but depreciation allowances on research property are treated as expenditures. (c)(1) election to "charge such expenditures to capital account" and amortize "ratably over such period of not less than 60 months".
- Rev. Proc. 2025-28 (released August 28, 2025), https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-28.pdf : section 2.04(2): "Section 174A(a) provides that, notwithstanding § 263, a deduction is allowed" "for any domestic research or experimental expenditures which are paid or incurred by the taxpayer during the taxable year." Section 2.04(8): "any amount paid or incurred in connection with the development of any software is treated as a research or experimental expenditure." Section 2.04(9): the amendments "apply to amounts paid or incurred in taxable years beginning after December 31, 2024." On individuals: "For an individual reporting income or loss from a business, the return required by § 6012(a)(1) is the Form 1040 and a corresponding Schedule C." Small business taxpayers (average annual gross receipts under the 448(c) test) "may elect to apply § 174A(a) to deduct domestic research or experimental expenditures" retroactively to tax years beginning after December 31, 2021. The procedure does not mention cloud, hosting, or GPUs.
- Notice 2023-63 (interim guidance under TCJA section 174; still the only IRS description of what "software development" means), https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-23-63.pdf :
  - Section 4.03(1)(c), costs that are research expenditures: "Depreciation, amortization, or depletion allowances with respect to property used in the performance of SRE activities".
  - Section 4.03(1)(e): "Rent, utilities, insurance, taxes, repairs and maintenance costs, security costs, and similar overhead costs with respect to facilities, equipment and other assets" "used in the performance of SRE activities or in the direct support of SRE activities."
  - Section 4.03(2)(e), costs that are not research expenditures: "Costs for website hosting that involve the payment of a specified, periodic fee to an Internet service provider in return for hosting a website on its server(s) connected to the Internet".
  - Section 5.03, software development activities: "Planning the development of the computer software" "Designing the computer software" "Building a model of the computer software" "Writing source code and converting it to machine-readable code" "Testing the computer software" "but only up until the point in time that" "In the case of computer software developed for use by the taxpayer in its trade or business, the computer software is placed in service".
  - Section 5.05(1), not software development for software used in the taxpayer's business: "Training employees and other stakeholders that will use the computer software" "Maintenance activities after the computer software is placed in service that do not give rise to upgrades and enhancements" "Data conversion activities" "Installing the computer software and other activities relating to placing the computer software in service."
  - Section 5.04: "the purchase and installation of purchased computer software, including the configuration of pre-coded parameters to make such software compatible with the business" "are not activities that constitute software development for purposes of § 174."
  - The word "cloud" appears once (section 5.02(1), quoted in section 1 above). "GPU" does not appear.

Estimates on how this applies:
- Compute used to write, build and test your serving stack before you open it to customers is a 174A cost (by analogy to "Rent, utilities ... equipment ... used in the performance of SRE activities"). Compute used to run the stack for customers after it is placed in service is 162. Compute used only to deploy an open-weight model with configuration changes looks like "configuration of pre-coded parameters" (section 5.04), which is not development, so it is 162.
- Year of deduction is the same (paid) under both sections for a domestic cash-basis sole proprietor, so misclassifying does not change 2025 or 2026 tax unless the research is "foreign research" (then 174 requires 15-year amortization). Whether running development jobs on GPUs physically located outside the US makes the research "conducted outside the United States" under 41(d)(4)(F) is not addressed by any guidance found; keep development compute in US regions to avoid the question.
- A new business that starts in 2025 or later adopts 174A(a) deduction as its method on its first return; no Form 3115 is needed (Rev. Proc. 2025-28 method-change procedures are for taxpayers with pre-2025 capitalized costs).

## 4. GPU hardware depreciation

Takeaway: GPUs, GPU servers and their peripherals are "qualified technological equipment" under IRC 168(i)(2) and therefore 5-year property under 168(e)(3)(B)(iv) (asset class 00.12, Information Systems). They qualify for 100% bonus depreciation under 168(k) if acquired after January 19, 2025 and placed in service after that date (no written binding contract on or before that date). Computers are no longer listed property, so the "more than 50% business use" listed-property record rules do not apply, although partial personal use still reduces the depreciable basis. The IRS has said nothing specific about GPUs or AI hardware. Its only hardware-adjacent statements are Notice 2014-21 (mining can be a trade or business). No Tax Court opinion on mining-equipment depreciation was found.

Primary sources:

- IRC 168(e)(3)(B)(iv), https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168 : 5-year property includes "any qualified technological equipment". IRC 168(i)(2)(A): the term means "any computer or peripheral equipment" and two other categories; (i)(2)(B)(ii): "computer" means "a programmable electronically activated device which" accepts information, applies prescribed processes and supplies results.
- Rev. Proc. 87-56, asset class 00.12 (as reproduced in the Thomson Reuters MACRS asset life table), https://thomsonreuters.com/en-us/help/fixed-assets-cs/screens/hid_help_asset_lives : "Information systems include computers and their peripheral equipment used in administering normal business transactions" "the maintenance of business records, their retrieval and analysis." Class life 6 years; GDS recovery 5 years; ADS 5 years. The table's footnote says qualified technological equipment as defined in section 168(i)(2) gets a 5-year recovery period regardless of class life. (Pub 946 Appendix B Table B-1 is published as an image, so the IRS text was not quotable directly.)
- IRS Pub 946 (2025), https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946 : 5-year GDS list includes "Any qualified technological equipment." ADS table: "Computers and peripheral equipment" "5 years". What's New: P.L. 119-21 "reinstated the 100% special depreciation allowance for certain qualified property" acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025; "you can elect to take a 40% special depreciation allowance (60% for long production period property and certain aircraft)" for the first tax year ending after January 19, 2025. Glossary: "Listed property: Passenger automobiles; any other property used for transportation; and property of a type generally used for entertainment, recreation, or amusement." Elsewhere: "certain listed property (such as computers placed in service before 2018)".
- IRC 168(k) as amended by P.L. 119-21 section 70301, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168 : (k)(1)(A): the depreciation deduction "shall include an allowance equal to 100 percent of the adjusted basis of the qualified property." (k)(2)(A)(i)(I): property "to which this section applies which has a recovery period of 20 years or less." (k)(2)(A)(ii): "the original use of which begins with the taxpayer or the acquisition of which by the taxpayer meets the requirements of clause (i) of subparagraph (E)" (used property qualifies if not from a related party and not previously used by the taxpayer). (k)(10)(A) transitional election: for property "placed in service by the taxpayer during the first taxable year ending after January 19, 2025," the taxpayer may elect "40 percent" (or "60 percent" for long-production property). Effective date note, section 70301(c): the amendments "shall apply to property acquired after January 19, 2025". The statute no longer contains a phase-down; the editorial note says P.L. 119-21 "struck out pars. (6) and (8) which defined the term 'applicable percentage.'"
- Notice 2026-11 (interim guidance on post-OBBBA bonus depreciation), https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-11.pdf : OBBBA section 70301 provides "a permanent 100 percent additional first year depreciation deduction for qualified property acquired and placed in service, and specified plants planted or grafted, after January 19, 2025." "property is not treated as acquired after the date a written binding contract is entered into for such acquisition." The notice also confirms taxpayers may elect 40 percent "instead of 100 percent" for the first taxable year ending after January 19, 2025.
- IRC 280F(d)(4), https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/280F : listed property now means "any passenger automobile," "any other property used as a means of transportation," "any property of a type generally used for purposes of entertainment, recreation, or amusement, and" "any other property of a type specified by the Secretary by regulations." - Tax Court cases (2023 to 2026) on crypto-mining equipment depreciation or section 183 for miners: not found. The closest recent crypto case is Paschall v. Commissioner, T.C. The closest recent section 183 case is Young v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2025-95 (ranching), covered in section 5.

Estimates: a GPU server bought new or used in 2026 and placed in service in 2026 for a Schedule C business is 100% deductible federally in 2026 under 168(k) with no election needed; electing out requires a statement with the return. Partial personal use (for example gaming) reduces the basis eligible for depreciation to the business-use percentage. If business use is 50% or less, section 179 is unavailable (Reg. 1.179-1(d)), but bonus has no 50% test for non-listed property. Bonus depreciation can create a Schedule C loss; section 179 cannot exceed business taxable income (which, for an individual, includes W-2 wages under Reg. 1.179-2(c)(6)(iv)). California will allow only $25,000 of section 179 and no bonus, so the state deduction is spread over 5 years.

## 5. The loss rules that drive audits of a high-income W-2 earner with a new Schedule C loss

Takeaway: The IRS attacks early-year Schedule C losses against wages in this order: (1) IRC 183, is this a business with an honest profit objective; (2) IRC 469, did you materially participate; (3) IRC 465, are you at risk for the money; (4) IRC 461(l), is the loss over $256,000 ($512,000 joint) in 2026; (5) IRC 6662 penalties and, rarely for a real operating business, IRC 7701(o). The IRS audit guide for 183 tells examiners to flag "Multiple several years with little or no income and large losses" and losses "offsetting other income", and to ask for the "idea", "business plan", "business bank account" and "business location". The regulation says a reasonable expectation of profit is not required and that start-up losses are expected. The Supreme Court says a tax motive does not disqualify a real transaction. A business that charges customers, prices to make money, keeps books, and can show a path to profit survives; a loss machine with no customers does not.

Section 183 and the audit guide:

- IRC 183, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/183 : (a) for an activity not engaged in for profit "no deduction attributable to such activity shall be allowed under this chapter except as provided in this section." (c): such an activity is "any activity other than one with respect to which deductions are allowable for the taxable year" "under section 162 or under paragraph (1) or (2) of section 212." (d) presumption: "If the gross income derived from an activity for 3 or more of the taxable years" "in the period of 5 consecutive taxable years which ends with the taxable year exceeds the deductions" the activity "shall be presumed for purposes of this chapter for such taxable year to be an activity engaged in for profit." (e): the taxpayer may elect that the determination "not be made before the close of the fourth taxable year" after the activity begins (Form 5213).
- Reg. 1.183-2, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.183-2 : (a) "Although a reasonable expectation of profit is not required, the facts and circumstances must indicate that the taxpayer" "entered into the activity, or continued the activity, with the objective of making a profit." "it may be sufficient that there is a small chance of making a large profit." (b) nine factors: "Manner in which the taxpayer carries on the activity." "The expertise of the taxpayer or his advisors." "The time and effort expended by the taxpayer in carrying on the activity." "Expectation that assets used in activity may appreciate in value." "The success of the taxpayer in carrying on other similar or dissimilar activities." "The taxpayer's history of income or losses with respect to the activity." "The amount of occasional profits, if any, which are earned." "The financial status of the taxpayer." "Elements of personal pleasure or recreation." "No one factor is determinative in making this determination." (b)(6): "A series of losses during the initial or start-up stage of an activity may not necessarily be an indication" "that the activity is not engaged in for profit." But losses "beyond the period which customarily is necessary to bring the operation to profitable status" that are not explained by "customary business risks or reverses, may be indicative that the activity is not being engaged in for profit." (b)(8): "Substantial income from sources other than the activity" "(particularly if the losses from the activity generate substantial tax benefits)" "may indicate that the activity is not engaged in for profit."
- IRS Audit Technique Guide, "Activities Not Engaged in for Profit, Internal Revenue Code Section 183" (Publication 5558, Rev. 09/2021), https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5558.pdf :
  - Disclaimer: "This document is not an official pronouncement of the law or the position of the Service and cannot be" "used, cited, or relied upon as such."
  - Purpose of 183: Congress wanted "to determine whether a taxpayer was engaged in an activity for the purpose of" "making a profit or just attempting to create and utilize losses to offset other" "unrelated income. Congress was requiring that the taxpayer have an 'honest objective' of making a profit rather than a 'reasonable expectation'".
  - What examiners look for ("red flags"): "The nature of the business or activity." "Multiple several years with little or no income and large losses." "Minimal income and large expenses for several years and those losses" "are offsetting other income." "The business or activity includes elements of recreation and/or personal pleasure."
  - Targeting: TIGTA "recommended that the IRS: (1) use its research capabilities to identify" "high-income individual returns with multiyear Schedule C losses and other" "factors that indicate the taxpayer may not have a profit objective for the activity". TIGTA "estimated that about 1.5 million" "taxpayers claimed net losses on Schedule C's that reduced their income from" "other sources in each of four consecutive years between 2002 and 2005."
  - Questions examiners ask: "Examiners should inquire into the taxpayer's: (1) idea, (2) business plan, (3)" "business bank account, and (4) business location."
  - Start-up phase: "A taxpayer may have an intent to make a profit but be losing money because" "his activity is still within its start-up phase. Some activities have start-up phases" "that last several years." "If it is determined that the activity has gone beyond its start-up phase," examiners should "cite a" "reputable industry source for the start-up phase."
  - Financial status: "if the taxpayer has substantial" "income from other sources and does not rely on the profitability of the activity to" "sustain their livelihood, then a profit objective may not be present." "if a taxpayer quit another activity (e.g., job as a W-2 employee) so" "that they could devote more time and effort the activity, it would suggest that a" "profit objective is present."
  - Presumption: "Section 183(d) gives a taxpayer a presumption of profit intent if gross income" "from an activity exceeds the deductions from the activity for at least 3 out of 5" "taxable years ending with the relevant taxable year."
- IRS Fact Sheet FS-2008-23 (June 2008, archived), https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/is-your-hobby-a-for-profit-endeavor : "An activity is presumed for profit if it makes a profit in at least three of the last five tax years". Questions: "Does the time and effort put into the activity indicate an intention to make a profit?" "Do you depend on income from the activity?" "did they occur in the start-up phase of the business?" "Have you changed methods of operation to improve profitability?" "Do you have the knowledge needed to carry on the activity as a successful business?"
- Young v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2025-95, as summarized by Current Federal Tax Developments (September 22, 2025), https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2025/9/22/profit-motive-under-section-183-an-analysis-of-young-v-commissioner : ranch losses disallowed; "The court found the Youngs’ operations were not businesslike during the years at issue." They "failed to maintain a general ledger despite Ms. Young’s accounting experience". "The Youngs had substantial income from ETI and wages, which allowed them to sustain the ranch’s losses." "The tax benefits generated by the losses were significant, offsetting their other high income."

Section 461(l), excess business loss:

- IRC 461(l), https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/461 : "any excess business loss of the taxpayer for the taxable year shall not be allowed." (l)(2): "Any loss which is disallowed under paragraph (1) shall be treated as a net operating loss for the taxable year" for carryover under 172(b). (l)(3)(A): the excess of "the aggregate deductions of the taxpayer for the taxable year which are attributable to trades or businesses" over business income plus "$250,000 (200 percent of such amount in the case of a joint return)." "Such excess shall be determined without regard to any deductions, gross income, or gains" "attributable to any trade or business of performing services as an employee." (l)(3)(C): indexed from 2026 by substituting "2024" for "2016".
- Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 3.31, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf : "For taxable years beginning in 2026, in" "determining a taxpayer's excess business loss, the amount under § 461(l)(3)(A)(ii)(II) is" "$256,000 ($512,000 for joint returns)."
- Instructions for Form 461, https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i461 : the One Big Beautiful Bill Act "permanently extended the disallowance of a deduction for excess business losses." "For 2025, the threshold amount is $313,000 ($626,000 for taxpayers filing a joint return)." The disallowed amount "is treated as a net operating loss (NOL) carryover for subsequent years."

Section 465, at risk:

- IRC 465, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/465 : a loss "shall be allowed only to the extent of the aggregate amount" the taxpayer is at risk. At risk includes "the amount of money and the adjusted basis of other property contributed by the taxpayer to the activity" and borrowed amounts to the extent the taxpayer "is personally liable for the repayment of such amounts." Not at risk: amounts "protected against loss" through "nonrecourse financing, guarantees, stop loss agreements, or other similar arrangements." The section applies to "each activity" the taxpayer engages in "in carrying on a trade or business or for the production of income". Estimate: GPUs bought with cash or a personally guaranteed loan are fully at risk; vendor financing with no personal liability is not.

Section 469, passive activity and material participation:

- IRC 469, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/469 : (a)(1) neither "the passive activity loss, nor" "the passive activity credit, for the taxable year shall be allowed." (c)(1): a passive activity is any activity "which involves the conduct of any trade or business, and" "in which the taxpayer does not materially participate." (c)(2): "the term 'passive activity' includes any rental activity." (h)(1): material participation requires involvement that is "regular," "continuous," and "substantial." (j)(8): "The term 'rental activity' means any activity where payments are principally for the use of tangible property."
- Reg. 1.469-5T(a), https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.469-5T : test 1: "The individual participates in the activity for more than 500 hours during such year;" test 2: the individual's participation "constitutes substantially all of the participation in such activity of all individuals"; test 3: "participates in the activity for more than 100 hours during the taxable year" and "is not less than the participation in the activity of any other individual"; test 7: "Based on all of the facts and circumstances" "the individual participates in the activity on a regular, continuous, and substantial basis during such year." (f)(4): "The extent of an individual's participation in an activity may be established by any reasonable means." "Contemporaneous daily time reports, logs, or similar documents are not required if the extent of such participation may be established by other reasonable means." Reasonable means include hours estimated "based on appointment books, calendars, or narrative summaries."
- Estimate: a one-person inference business where the owner does all the work meets test 2 (substantially all participation) even with few hours, so the loss is nonpassive. Danger: if the business is framed as renting out GPUs by the hour (payments "principally for the use of tangible property"), it is a "rental activity" and per se passive under 469(c)(2) unless a Reg. 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii) exception applies (for example average customer use of 7 days or less). Selling inference as a service avoids this. Keep a time log anyway.

Economic substance and penalties:

- IRC 7701(o), https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701 : a transaction has economic substance only if "the transaction changes in a meaningful way (apart from Federal income tax effects) the taxpayer's economic position," and "the taxpayer has a substantial purpose (apart from Federal income tax effects) for entering into such transaction." (o)(5)(C): "The determination of whether the economic substance doctrine is relevant to a transaction" "shall be made in the same manner as if this subsection had never been enacted."
- IRS memo LB&I-04-0422-0014 (April 22, 2022), as reported in The Tax Adviser (January 2023), https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2023/jan/memo-removes-irs-procedural-requirements-for-economic-substance-arguments/ : the memo changed procedure "by removing the previously required four-step process for relying on the economic substance doctrine" "including the requirement for executive approval." Examiners now need only "approval from their immediate supervisor" for the penalty, consistent with "the requirement under Sec. 6751(b) that penalties be timely approved in writing by the immediate supervisor". The IRS memo itself was not retrievable from irs.gov (not found online).
- IRC 6662, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662 : "there shall be added to the tax an amount equal to 20 percent of the portion of the underpayment" attributable to "Negligence or disregard of rules or regulations." "Any substantial understatement of income tax." "Any disallowance of claimed tax benefits by reason of a transaction lacking economic substance". Negligence "includes any failure to make a reasonable attempt to comply with the provisions of this title". Substantial understatement for an individual: the understatement "exceeds the greater of" "10 percent of the tax required to be shown on the return for the taxable year, or" "$5,000." (i): for nondisclosed noneconomic substance transactions the penalty is applied "by substituting '40 percent' for '20 percent'."
- Estimate: 7701(o) targets structured transactions. For an operating business that buys compute and sells inference to real customers, the live issue is 183 and 469, not 7701(o). The 6662 negligence penalty is the normal add-on if 183 deductions are disallowed.

Is a "tax play" or loss leader permissible? Supreme Court and IRS position:

- Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/293/465 : "The legal right of a taxpayer to decrease the amount of what otherwise would be his taxes, or altogether avoid them," "by means which the law permits, cannot be doubted." "But the question for determination is whether what was done," "apart from the tax motive, was the thing which the statute intended."
- Frank Lyon Co. v. United States, 435 U.S. 561 (1978), https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/435/561 : "where, as here, there is a genuine multiple-party transaction with economic substance" "which is compelled or encouraged by business or regulatory realities, is imbued with tax-independent considerations," "and is not shaped solely by tax-avoidance features that have meaningless labels attached," "the Government should honor the allocation of rights and duties effectuated by the parties." "The fact that favorable tax consequences were taken into account by Lyon on entering into the transaction" "is no reason for disallowing those consequences."
- IRS position in its own words: Reg. 1.183-2(a) ("a reasonable expectation of profit is not required"; "a small chance of making a large profit" can suffice) and the ATG ("honest objective" standard; start-up losses expected). Estimate: the IRS has no published statement blessing a "loss leader" as such. Its rules allow losses when the activity is run to make money. A plan whose only economics are the tax savings fails 183 and, if structured, 7701(o).

## 6. Licenses, registration, and California sales tax on AI inference

Takeaway: No federal or California license is required to sell AI inference as a service. Selling inference is not money transmission (you accept payment for your own service), not telecommunications (estimate), and not covered by California's frontier-AI law unless you train a 10^26-operation model. You need the ordinary items: a city business tax certificate (estimate; requirements vary by city), and from January 1, 2027 a CDTFA seller's permit and sales tax collection if your product is a taxable "digital product" under SB 122. Through December 31, 2026, California does not tax SaaS or remotely accessed software. Whether an inference API is a taxable SaaS "digital product" or excluded "digital infrastructure" (IaaS/PaaS) from 2027 is not yet settled; CDTFA's emergency regulations are in process.

Primary sources:

- US Small Business Administration, "Apply for licenses and permits", https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits : "Business activities regulated by a federal agency will need a federal license or permit." The federal list covers agriculture, alcoholic beverages, aviation, firearms, fish and wildlife, commercial fisheries, maritime transportation, mining and drilling, nuclear energy, radio and television broadcasting, and transportation and logistics. "The licenses and permits you need from the state, county, or city will depend on your business activities" and location. Software and AI services are not on the federal list.
- Money transmission, 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5), https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100 : money transmission is "the acceptance of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes" "for currency from one person and the transmission of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency" "to another location or person by any means." Exclusion (ff)(5)(ii)(F): a person who "Accepts and transmits funds only integral to the sale of goods or the provision of services," "other than money transmission services, by the person who is accepting and transmitting the funds." Estimate: selling prepaid inference credits for your own service falls in this exclusion. A California Money Transmission Act license is likewise not triggered by selling your own service (estimate; DFPI not consulted).
- Telecom license: not found. No FCC or CPUC source treats an application-layer API as a telecommunications service. Estimate: an inference API is an information service, not telecommunications.
- California SB 53 (Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act), as explained by the Future of Privacy Forum, https://fpf.org/blog/californias-sb-53-the-first-frontier-ai-law-explained/ : "SB 53 regulates developers of the most advanced and resource-intensive AI models," meaning "foundation models trained with more than 10^26 computational operations". A frontier developer is one that "trained or initiated the training" of such a model; large frontier developers are "those with annual gross revenues above $500 million." A reseller of inference on existing models is outside these definitions (estimate).
- CDTFA Publication 107, seller's permit, https://www.cdtfa.ca.gov/formspubs/pub107/ : "Generally, if you make sales of tangible personal property in California, you are required to hold a seller's permit" unless an exemption applies.
- CDTFA Regulation 1502 (current through 2026), https://www.cdtfa.ca.gov/lawguides/vol1/sutr/1502.html : (f)(1)(D): "The sale or lease of a prewritten program is not a taxable transaction if the program is transferred" "by remote telecommunications from the seller's place of business, to or through the purchaser's computer" and "the purchaser does not obtain possession of any tangible personal property". (c)(7): charges "for the use of a computer, on a time-sharing basis" accessed by remote telecommunication are not taxable. Last amended December 17, 2013, effective July 1, 2014. Estimate: under this regulation, selling AI inference through 2026 is a nontaxable service in California.
- SB 122 (Stats. 2026, ch. 23), signed June 29, 2026, effective January 1, 2027, as summarized by K&L Gates (October 8, 2026), https://www.klgates.com/thought-leadership/California-Expands-Sales-and-Use-Tax-to-SaaS-and-Certain-Digital-Products-Starting-1-January-2027-CDTFA-Proposes-Emergency-Regulations-to-Implement-the-New-Law-10-8-2026 : "software-as-a-service (SaaS), other remotely accessed software, and digital products" "will become subject to California sales and use tax." Statutory sections include Revenue and Taxation Code 6006, 6010.5.1, 6372 and 6372.1. "Tax does not apply to custom computer software (other than a 'basic operational program') regardless of transfer form." The definition excludes "digital infrastructure (i.e., IaaS/PaaS)". A service provided electronically is exempt "where the service primarily involves the application of human effort by the service provider" (RTC 6372.1). Sales to a single purchaser that "exceed US$5,000,000 in the aggregate in the current calendar year" shift the tax to the purchaser.
- CDTFA, Retailers and Purchasers of Digital Products, https://cdtfa.ca.gov/industry/retailers-and-purchasers-of-digital-products/retailers.htm : "Retailers selling digital products for use in California are generally required to:" "Register with CDTFA". "a digital product that represents a service provided in electronic form is exempt from sales and use tax". Purchaser threshold: purchases that "exceed $5,000,000 in the 2027 calendar year".
- CDTFA definitions page, https://cdtfa.ca.gov/industry/retailers-and-purchasers-of-digital-products/definitions.htm : "A digital product is a prewritten computer software transferred on tangible storage media," "transferred electronically, or accessed remotely." SaaS: "Software as a service (SaaS) involving the right to access and use the provider's prewritten computer software" "running on a cloud infrastructure or the right to access that prewritten computer software from various client devices" "through either a thin client interface, including a web browser, or a program interface". Not a digital product: "digital infrastructure", defined as "A cloud-based service provided remotely that allows a user to create, deploy, scale, or run the user's own" "computer software on the service provider's digital platform"; "Examples include cloud-based Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) and Platform as a Service (PaaS)".

Estimate on 2027: an inference API that returns model output to customers through "a program interface" matches the SaaS definition more closely than the "digital infrastructure" exclusion, because the customer runs your model, not the customer's own software. Expect to register with CDTFA and collect California sales tax from California customers from January 1, 2027 unless the final emergency regulations carve out AI APIs. The GPU rental you buy from a US cloud provider is "digital infrastructure" (IaaS) and should stay untaxed in California.
